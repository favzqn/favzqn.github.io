# The Pomodoro Technique

A time management method developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s: work for 25 minutes with total focus, then take a 5-minute break. After four cycles, take a longer break (15-30 minutes). Named after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer Cirillo used as a university student.

---

## The Method

1. Choose a task
2. Set a timer for 25 minutes
3. Work with complete focus until the timer rings
4. Take a 5-minute break
5. Repeat
6. After four pomodoros, take a longer break

The simplicity is the point. The technique removes decision fatigue — you don't decide how long to work, you just start the timer.

---

## Why It Works

Barbara Oakley in [[A Mind for Numbers]] recommends the Pomodoro as the primary tool for overcoming procrastination. The 25-minute commitment is small enough to bypass the brain's resistance ("I can do anything for 25 minutes") but long enough to produce meaningful work.

The breaks are equally important. They allow the [[Focused and Diffuse Modes]] to cycle — the focused work builds the initial understanding, and the break lets the diffuse mode make connections. This is why stepping away from a problem often produces the "aha" moment.

---

## The Procrastination Connection

Procrastination is not laziness — it is an emotional regulation strategy. The brain avoids tasks that trigger discomfort (anxiety, boredom, self-doubt). The Pomodoro works because it reframes the task: instead of "write the whole report," it's "just do 25 minutes." The emotional barrier shrinks.
