# Chapter 4: Social Proof — Truths Are Us

Detailed chapter on how we use others' behavior to determine our own.

---

## Core Principle

When people are free to do as they please, they usually imitate one another. We look to others to determine correct behavior.

---

## Key Experiments & Stories

### Beijing Restaurant Experiment
- Labeling dishes as "most popular" → sales jumped 13-20%
- Dishes became popular BECAUSE of their popularity
- Costless, ethical, easy to implement

### London Pub Experiment
- Sign: "most popular beer this week was porter"
- Porter sales **doubled immediately**

### McFlurry at McDonald's
- "The McFlurry is our visitors' favorite" → sales jumped **55%**
- After ordering, "X flavor is our visitors' favorite topping" → **48% more toppings**

### Netflix Popularity Data
- Netflix started sharing viewing numbers publicly
- "Popularity precipitates popularity"
- Internal tests showed sharing popularity increased viewing

### Solomon Asch Conformity Experiments
- People conformed to obviously wrong answers when group agreed
- ~75% conformed at least once
- Group pressure overrides individual judgment

### Milgram Obedience Experiments
- People administered (fake) shocks when ordered by authority
- 65% delivered maximum voltage
- Shows power of authority + social proof

---

## When Social Proof Backfires

### Planned Parenthood
- Donation requests: "your friends have donated"
- Result: donations DECREASED
- People thought they'd already given

### Suicide Reporting
- Publicizing suicide methods increases similar suicides (Werther effect)
- Media coverage of celebrity suicides → spike in imitations

---

## Defense

- Check if the "proof" is genuine or manufactured
- Consider whether the group is knowledgeable
- Popularity ≠ quality
- Be wary of "everyone is doing it" arguments
