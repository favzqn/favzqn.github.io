# Chapter 6: Scarcity — The Rule of the Few

Detailed chapter on why we want what is rare or disappearing.

---

## Core Principle

Opportunities seem more valuable when they are less available. We want what we can't have.

---

## Key Experiments & Stories

### The Divorce Lawyer's Wording
- "All you have to do is agree" → fails
- "We have a deal. All you have to do is agree" → succeeds
- Framing makes the offer seem like something about to be lost

### Florida State Cafeteria Study
- Students rated cafeteria food as unsatisfactory
- 9 days later, after a fire closed the cafeteria for 2 weeks
- Students rated the SAME food significantly better
- Losing access made it more desirable

### iPhone 5 Launch
- Woman traded a $2,800 Louis Vuitton bag for a spot in line
- "I heard that this store didn't have a big supply, and I didn't want to risk losing the chance"
- Scarcity drives extreme behavior

---

## The Two Key Triggers

### 1. Scarcity of Information
- "New!" (first-time availability) → more persuasive
- Old information (even if identical) loses persuasive power
- Explains why "new and improved" works

### 2. Scarcity of Availability
- "Limited time!" / "Only 3 left!" → urgency
- Restrictions on freedom → desire increases (reactance)
- Forbidden fruit effect

---

## Psychological Reactance

When our freedom to choose is threatened, we want the restricted thing MORE. This is a deep psychological need for autonomy.

- Adolescents told they can't read a book → wanted to read it more
- Censored information → perceived as more valuable
- "You can't have this" → "I must have it"

---

## How It's Exploited

- "Limited time offer"
- "Only 2 left in stock"
- Flash sales and countdown timers
- "Exclusive" access
- "One-time only" pricing
- Doorbuster deals

---

## Defense

- Ask: "Would I want this if it were readily available?"
- Separate genuine scarcity from manufactured urgency
- Pause before acting on "limited time" pressure
- Scarcity doesn't change the underlying value
