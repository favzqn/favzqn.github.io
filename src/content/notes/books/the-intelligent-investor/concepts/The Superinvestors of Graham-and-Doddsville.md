# The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville

Warren Buffett's famous 1984 Columbia University speech, included as Appendix 1 in The Intelligent Investor.

---

## The Argument

Efficient-market theorists claim that anyone who beats the market year after year is just lucky — like a coin-flipper who gets heads ten times in a row. Buffett counters by presenting a group of investors, all trained by Graham, who consistently beat the market over decades.

The probability of this many people outperforming by pure chance is astronomically low. What they have in common is not a shared stock-picking system but a **shared intellectual framework**: buying value at a margin of safety.

---

## The Investors

Buffett describes nine value-investing funds managed by Graham-trained investors, all with above-market returns over 15+ year periods. None used the same approach — they bought different stocks, in different markets, at different times. What they shared was Graham's philosophy.

---

## The Implication

The efficient-market hypothesis is not wrong — it's incomplete. Markets are mostly efficient, but not always. The intelligent investor can exploit the gaps when they appear, but only with the right temperament and analytical framework.
