# The Science of Likability

*The Science of Likability: 27 Studies to Master Charisma, Attract Friends, Captivate People, and Take Advantage of Human Psychology* by Patrick King (2014).

---

## Summary

A psychology-grounded guide to likability organized around 27 studies across 14 chapters. King's approach: likability is not luck or innate talent — it's applied psychology. Each chapter presents research studies, explains the mechanisms, and gives actionable techniques.

### Ch 1: How to Improve People's Moods

**Eich, Macauley & Ryan (1994)**: Memory is context-dependent — memories are linked to environment, mood, and sensory details. Trigger someone's positive memory elements and they'll unconsciously slip into the mood they were in during that memory.

**Byrne & Clore (1970)**: If people are nearby when we feel good, we begin to associate THEM with good feelings (Pavlovian conditioning). Become the bell that makes people smile.

**Key**: Compliment people. Most people receive zero compliments daily — yours might be 100% of their daily intake.

### Ch 2: How to Turn Enemies into Friends

**Jecker & Landy**: Participants liked a researcher who asked them for a small favor MORE than one who didn't. The **Benjamin Franklin effect**: doing a favor for someone causes cognitive dissonance, which the brain resolves by deciding you must like them.

**Key**: Ask your enemies for small favors. Their brains will rationalize that they must like you.

### Ch 3: How to Create the Foundation of Friendship

**Equity Theory (Walster, Walster & Berscheid, 1978)**: Relationships are inherently transactional. People seek equality — they don't want to feel like predators or prey. Keeping mental score and maintaining equity makes you more likable.

**Similarity**: Byrne (1971) showed that the more similar someone's profile, the more attractive they rated them. Search for or CREATE similarities.

### Ch 4: How to Act the Part

**Freud's Transference**: If you act the part of a familiar friend, people will treat you like one — subconsciously.

**Pygmalion Effect (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968)**: Students who were randomly labeled "gifted" performed better because teachers gave them preferential treatment. People become who you expect them to be.

### Ch 5: How to Persuade and Negotiate Effectively

**Door-in-the-Face (DITF)**: Ask for something huge, get rejected, then ask for your real (smaller) goal. The comparison makes it seem reasonable.

**Foot-in-the-Door (FITD)**: Start with a tiny request, get compliance, then escalate. Saying yes once makes saying yes again easier.

### Ch 6: How to Gain Trust and Credibility

**Propinquity Effect**: Friendship increases linearly with simple interaction and exposure. The more we see someone, the more we trust them.

**Mere Exposure Effect (Zajonc, 1968)**: We prefer what's familiar. Repetition creates trust.

**Credibility indicators**: experience, qualifications, confidence, decisiveness, social proof.

### Ch 7: How to Work Well with Others

**Stimulus-Value-Role Model (Murstein, 1970)**: Three stages of friendship — stimulus (appearance/attraction), value (shared beliefs), role (working toward shared goals).

**Deberry (1989)**: Cooperation makes people like you; competition makes them compare. Focus on shared goals, not individual contributions.

### Ch 8: How to Be More Endearing

**Pratfall Effect (Aronson, 1966)**: Participants preferred the person who knocked over coffee. Vulnerability and imperfections make competent people MORE likable.

**Key**: Show your flaws. Superman is boring; flawed heroes are relatable.

### Ch 9: How to Convince People to Act

**Reactance Theory**: When freedom is restricted, people rebel. Tell people they CAN'T do something, and they'll want to do it. Reverse psychology works on adults too.

### Ch 10: How to Lead Anyone

**Six Leadership Styles (Goleman)**: Visionary, Coaching, Affiliative, Democratic, Pace-Setting, Commanding. Each works best in different contexts with different people.

### Ch 11: How to Avoid Being Judged

**Stereotypes fill information gaps**: When people don't know you, they fill in blanks with stereotypes. The antidote: provide details. Oversharing (positive/neutral) makes you less stereotyped and more likable.

**Aron et al. (1997)**: Strangers who asked each other sensitive personal questions developed greater trust and emotional closeness than those who didn't.

### Ch 12: How to Win Groups Over

**Three factors**: Number (fewer is easier), Immediacy (physical closeness), Strength (your importance to the group).

**Groupthink**: Groups default to agreement. Split groups into smaller factions to influence them individually.

### Ch 13: How to Be Funny and Charismatic

**Speed > Accuracy**: Quick responses are rated as more charismatic and intelligent than accurate but slow ones.

**Benign Moral Violations (McGraw & Warren, 2010)**: Humor works when something is immoral but harmless. Too immoral = offensive. Too benign = boring. The sweet spot = funny.

### Ch 14: How to Chit-Chat Effectively

**Dunbar (1998)**: Gossip is the human version of primate social grooming — it's how bonds are formed.

**Key**: Talk about social relationships. Positive gossip makes you look good; negative gossip makes you look like the evil you describe.

## Key Themes

1. **Likability Is Psychology, Not Luck** — every aspect can be controlled through understanding mechanisms
2. **Memory and Mood Are Linked** — trigger positive memories and people associate you with good feelings
3. **Vulnerability Is Strength** — the Pratfall effect shows that flaws increase likability
4. **Exposure Creates Trust** — propinquity and mere exposure turn strangers into friends
5. **Speed Beats Accuracy** — quick responses are perceived as confident and intelligent
6. **People Become Your Expectations** — the Pygmalion effect means you create the world you live in
7. **Gossip Is Social Bonding** — talking about others is how humans build relationships
