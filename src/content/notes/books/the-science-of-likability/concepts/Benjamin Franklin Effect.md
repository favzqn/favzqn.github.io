# Benjamin Franklin Effect

Asking someone for a favor makes them like you MORE — even if they previously disliked you.

---

## The Research

**Jecker and Landy**: Participants who were asked to return a researcher's money (a small favor) liked the researcher significantly more than those who weren't asked.

## The Mechanism

1. Your enemy does you a favor
2. Their brain experiences cognitive dissonance: "I hate this person, but I helped them"
3. The brain resolves the contradiction: "I must not hate them that much — after all, I helped them"
4. They conclude they actually like you

## How to Use It

- Ask enemies for small, non-burdensome favors
- Don't overdo it — keep requests reasonable
- Each favor creates cognitive dissonance that resolves in your favor
- Subsequent requests become easier

## Benjamin Franklin's Version

Franklin asked a rival to lend him a rare book. The rival obliged. They became friends. Franklin noted: "He that has once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another than he whom you yourself have obliged."

## Key Insight

> "Asking someone to perform a favor for you is also a subtle form of flattery. It implies that you think they have something you lack — knowledge, taste, resources."
