# Disqualified from Being Human

The literal translation of the Japanese title 人間失格 (*Ningen Shikkaku*). Not "no longer human" but **"disqualified as a human being."**

Yozo's conviction is that he alone lacks something essential to being human. Everyone else seems to know instinctively how to live — how to eat, work, love, argue, pursue money, maintain relationships. He watches them and cannot understand. "All I feel are the assaults of apprehension and terror at the thought that I am the only one who is entirely unlike the rest."

---

## The Evidence Yozo Cites

- He has never known hunger (not privilege — disconnection from bodily needs)
- He cannot understand why humans eat three meals a day
- He finds mealtime rituals inexplicable and threatening
- He cannot say "I don't like it" or choose between alternatives
- He cannot argue or justify himself — criticism strikes him "with the force of a thunderbolt"
- He has never desired to kill anyone — only to die himself
- He cannot love, despite being loved by many

---

## The Paradox

The narrator's epilogue reveals the opposite: "He was an angel." Yozo's self-assessment is catastrophically wrong. He sees his clowning as proof of inhumanity; others see it as charm. He sees his passivity as weakness; others experience it as gentleness. He sees his inability to argue as deficiency; others experience it as humility.

The tragedy is not that Yozo is disqualified. The tragedy is that he believes he is.
