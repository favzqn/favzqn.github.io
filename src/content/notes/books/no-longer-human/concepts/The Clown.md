# The Clown

Yozo's clowning is not humor. It is survival.

He invents the role as a child — wearing a red woolen sweater over his cotton kimono, performing wild Indian dances, writing absurd compositions. The purpose is not joy but erasure: "As long as I can make them laugh, it doesn't matter how, I'll be all right. If I succeed in that, the human beings probably won't mind it too much if I remain outside their lives."

The clown is a mask that serves two functions:
1. **Protection** — if everyone is laughing, no one is looking at his true self
2. **Exile** — the clown exists outside the human circle, which is where Yozo wants to be

---

## How It Works

Yozo observes human behavior the way an anthropologist observes a foreign tribe. He cannot understand why people eat three meals a day, why they work, why they love. Since he cannot participate, he performs participation. His clowning is "the last quest for love I was to direct at human beings."

The performance is exhausting. It requires "excruciating efforts within" to maintain "a smile which never deserted my lips." He becomes "an accomplished clown, a child who never spoke a single truthful word."

---

## The Cost

The clown succeeds. Everyone laughs. But the success is the failure — the more perfectly he performs, the more completely his real self is buried. He becomes what he performs. The gap between the clown and the person widens until it becomes unbridgeable.

> "I have never once actually experienced friendship. I have only the most painful recollections of my various acquaintances."

---

## The Double Life

Every moment of Yozo's life is a performance. At school, his report card is all A's except deportment (C or D) — the only honest metric. In the underground movement, he performs revolutionary zeal. With women, he performs sensitivity. With Flatfish, he performs gratitude. The clown is everywhere, and nowhere.
