# Fallacies of Presumption

Assuming what needs to be proved. The argument smuggles in its conclusion as a premise.

---

## Key Fallacies

### Petitio Principii (Begging the Question)
Assume the conclusion in the premise. "God exists because the Bible says so, and the Bible is the word of God." The conclusion (God exists) is assumed in the premise (Bible is the word of God).

### Circulus in Probando (Circular Reasoning)
The conclusion appears in the premises. "Freedom of speech is important because people should be able to speak freely."

### Complex Question
Embed an assumption in a question. "Have you stopped beating your wife?" (Assumes you were beating your wife.)

### Bifurcation (False Dilemma)
Present only two options when more exist. "You're either with us or against us." (There are many intermediate positions.)

### Accident / Dicto Simpliciter
Apply a general rule to a specific case where it doesn't apply. "Free speech means I can say anything." (But not yelling "fire" in a theater.)

### The Analogical Fallacy
Assume that because two things are similar in one respect, they're similar in others. "The state is like a human body." (It has no liver, pancreas, or waste-disposal mechanism.)

### Apriorism
Insist on a principle despite contrary evidence. "We don't need to look through your telescope, Mr Galileo. We know there cannot be more than seven heavenly bodies."

## Key Insight

> "Fallacies of presumption are the hardest to detect because the assumption is hidden. It's not stated — it's smuggled in. Detection requires asking: 'What is being assumed here?'"
