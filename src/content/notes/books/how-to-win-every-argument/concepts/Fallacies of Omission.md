# Fallacies of Omission

Leaving out what matters. The argument works because key information is absent.

---

## Key Fallacies

### One-Sided Assessment
Present only the evidence for your case, ignoring the other side. "This drug has been shown to help 70% of patients!" (But not mentioning the 30% who got worse.)

### Special Pleading
Apply rules to others but exempt yourself. "I know I said deadlines matter, but MY situation is different."

### Definitional Retreat
Change the definition of your terms when challenged. "I didn't say ALL politicians — I meant the ones in power." (After being shown counterexamples.)

### Concealed Quantification
Hide the scope of a claim. "Politicians are corrupt." (All? Some? A few?)

### Extensional Pruning
Narrow the scope of a claim after it's challenged. "Doctors say this is safe." (Which doctors?)

### Damning the Alternatives
Attack every alternative to your position, leaving yours as the default. "We can't do A because... We can't do B because... So we must do C." (Maybe there's a D.)

### Shifting the Burden of Proof
Demand the other side prove a negative. "Prove that ghosts DON'T exist." (The burden is on the person making the claim.)

### Unobtainable Perfection
Reject a solution because it's not perfect. "This new drug has side effects, so we shouldn't use it." (Compared to what? The status quo?)

### Trivial Objections
Focus on minor flaws to dismiss the whole argument. "Your data has a 0.1% error rate, so the entire study is invalid."

## Key Insight

> "What's NOT said is often more important than what IS said. Detection requires asking: 'What is being left out?'"
