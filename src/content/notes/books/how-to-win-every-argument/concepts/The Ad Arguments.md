# The Ad Arguments

Appeals to something other than logic. The most common and recognizable family of fallacies.

---

## The Complete List

| Latin Name | English | The Appeal |
|---|---|---|
| Ad hominem (abusive) | Against the person | Attack the arguer's character |
| Ad hominem (circumstantial) | Against circumstances | Attack the arguer's situation |
| Tu quoque | You too | "You did it too!" |
| Ad populum | To the people | Everyone believes it |
| Ad verecundiam | To false authority | An "expert" says so |
| Ad misericordiam | To pity | Feel sorry for me |
| Ad crumenam | To the purse | Money/power says so |
| Ad baculum | To the force | Threats |
| Ad ignorantiam | To ignorance | Can't disprove it, so it's true |
| Ad lapidem | To absurdity | It's absurd, so it's wrong |
| Ad nauseam | To nausea | Repeat until believed |
| Ad antiquitam | To tradition | It's old, so it's good |
| Ad novitam | To novelty | It's new, so it's good |
| Ad numeram | To numbers | Many people do it |

## How They Map to Cialdini

- Ad populum = **Social Proof** ("everyone does it")
- Ad verecundiam = **Authority** ("an expert says so")
- Ad misericordiam = **Reciprocity** (appeal to pity triggers obligation)
- Ad baculum = **Scarcity** (threats create urgency)
- Ad antiquitam = **Commitment and Consistency** ("we've always done it")

## Key Insight

The ad arguments work because they tap into psychological biases that Cialdini documented. They're not logically valid, but they're psychologically powerful. Detection requires separating the emotional trigger from the logical content.
