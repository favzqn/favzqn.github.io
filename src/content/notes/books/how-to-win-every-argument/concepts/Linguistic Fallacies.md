# Linguistic Fallacies

Tricks of language that allow a statement to convey a meaning different from what it literally says.

---

## The 6 Linguistic Fallacies

### Accent
Changing the stress on words changes the meaning. "We should not speak ILL of our friends" implies we should speak ill of them in other ways. Authorized emphasis brings unauthorized implications.

### Amphiboly
Grammatical ambiguity creates multiple interpretations. "The Duchess has a fine ship, but she has barnacles on her bottom." Does "she" refer to the ship or the Duchess? Oracles and fortune-tellers love amphibolies — they can claim whichever meaning comes true.

### Composition
What is true of the parts is not necessarily true of the whole. "Every player on the team is excellent. Therefore the team is excellent." (Individual stars don't guarantee team success.)

### Division
The reverse of composition — what is true of the whole is not necessarily true of the parts. "The team is excellent. Therefore every player is excellent."

### Equivocation
Shifting the meaning of a word mid-argument. "The sign said 'fine for parking here,' so I parked." (Fine as in penalty, not permission.) Equivocation is the most versatile linguistic fallacy.

### Reification
Treating an abstraction as if it were concrete. "Nature intended women to be homemakers." (Nature doesn't "intend" anything.)

## Key Insight

Linguistic fallacies exploit the gap between what is said and what is meant. Detection requires listening to the STRUCTURE of the language, not just the content.
