# Circular Reasoning

The conclusion appears in the premises. You assume what you're trying to prove.

---

## Two Forms

### Petitio Principii (Begging the Question)
The conclusion is assumed in a premise. "God exists because the Bible says so, and the Bible is the word of God." The conclusion (God exists) is smuggled into the premise.

### Circulus in Probando (Circular Argument)
The conclusion IS the premise, restated. "Freedom of speech is important because people should be able to speak freely."

## Example

> "We know the witness is telling the truth because she's an honest person. We know she's honest because she always tells the truth."

## Why It Works

The circularity is often hidden by using different words for the same thing. The audience doesn't notice that the conclusion and premise are the same claim.

## How to Defend

Ask: "How do you know that premise is true?" If the answer is "because the conclusion is true," you've found the circle.
