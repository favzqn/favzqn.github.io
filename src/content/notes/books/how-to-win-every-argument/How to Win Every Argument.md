# How to Win Every Argument

*How to Win Every Argument: The Use and Abuse of Logic* by Madsen Pirie (2006). An encyclopedic reference of 70+ logical fallacies, each with definition, examples, and instructions for both detection and deployment.

---

## Summary

A comprehensive catalog of logical fallacies organized alphabetically, covering formal errors in logical structure, informal linguistic tricks, and three types of informal relevance fallacies (omission, intrusion, presumption). Each entry defines the fallacy, gives examples, explains why it's fallacious, and recommends how to use it "with maximum effect."

### The Five Categories

**1. Formal Fallacies** — errors in logical structure
- Affirming the consequent, denying the antecedent, undistributed middle, exclusive premises, illicit process, false conversion, quaternio terminorum, existential fallacy, positive conclusion from negative premises, conclusion which denies premises, contradictory premises

**2. Informal Linguistic Fallacies** — tricks of language
- Accent, amphiboly, composition, division, equivocation, reification

**3. Fallacies of Relevance (Omission)** — leaving out what matters
- Straw man, red herring, shifting the burden of proof, ad ignorantiam, ad lapidem, ad nauseam, one-sided assessment, special pleading, definitional retreat, concealed quantification, extensional pruning, damning the alternatives, refuting the example, shifting ground, trivial objections, unaccepted enthymemes, unobtainable perfection, the exception that proves the rule, bogus dilemma

**4. Fallacies of Relevance (Intrusion)** — introducing what doesn't belong
- Ad hominem (abusive and circumstantial), tu quoque, ad populum, ad verecundiam, ad misericordiam, ad crumenam, ad baculum, emotional appeals, blinding with science, loaded words, poisoning the well, irrelevant humour, the red herring, the runaway train, the slippery slope, wishful thinking

**5. Fallacies of Relevance (Presumption)** — assuming what needs proving
- Petitio principii, circulus in probando, complex question, accident, dicto simpliciter, secundum quid, analogical fallacy, apriorism, bifurcation, ad antiquitam, ad novitam, ad numeram, cum hoc ergo propter hoc, post hoc ergo propter hoc, ex-post-facto statistics, non-anticipation, hedging, half-concealed qualification, Thatcher's blame

### Key Fallacy Families

**The Ad Arguments** (appeals to something other than logic):
- Ad hominem — attack the person
- Ad populum — appeal to popularity
- Ad verecundiam — appeal to false authority
- Ad misericordiam — appeal to pity
- Ad crumenam — appeal to money
- Ad baculum — appeal to force
- Ad ignorantiam — appeal to ignorance
- Ad lapidem — appeal to absurdity
- Ad nauseam — appeal to repetition
- Ad antiquitam — appeal to tradition
- Ad novitam — appeal to novelty
- Ad numeram — appeal to numbers

**The Causal Fallacies**:
- Post hoc ergo propter hoc — after this, therefore because of this
- Cum hoc ergo propter hoc — correlation as causation
- Ex-post-facto statistics — statistics after the fact

**The Straw Man Family**:
- Straw man — misrepresent the argument
- Refuting the example — attack the weakest example
- Poisoning the well — discredit before they speak

## Key Themes

1. **Fallacies Are Tools** — Pirie doesn't just teach detection; he teaches deployment. Each entry includes how to use the fallacy to deceive.
2. **Latin Tags Matter** — naming a fallacy in Latin gives the accusation authority. "You're committing an ad hominem" hits harder than "you're attacking me."
3. **The Five Categories** — formal (structure), linguistic (language), omission (leaving out), intrusion (adding in), presumption (assuming)
4. **Sound Reasoning Wins** — but knowing when reasoning is unsound is equally powerful
5. **Everyone Uses Fallacies** — politicians, advertisers, colleagues, family. Detection is a survival skill.
