# The Omnivore's Dilemma

*The Omnivore's Dilemma: A Natural History of Four Meals* by Michael Pollan (2006).

---

## Summary

Pollan traces four meals through their food chains to understand the American way of eating. The book is structured as four parts, each following a different food chain from source to plate.

### Part 1: Industrial — Corn
The American food chain begins with corn. Pollan follows a bushel of corn from George Naylor's farm in Iowa through the feedlot (Poky Feeders in Kansas, where he tracks a steer he purchased), the processing plant (where corn becomes HFCS, maltodextrin, and dozens of other derivatives), and finally to a McDonald's meal he eats with his family.

Key findings:
- 97% of what a corn plant is comes from the air; 3% from the ground
- A McDonald's meal is overwhelmingly corn: soda (100%), milkshake (78%), chicken nuggets (56%), cheeseburger (52%), fries (23%)
- Feedlot cattle suffer from acidosis, liver abscesses, and require antibiotics to survive
- Government corn subsidies have created a surplus that distorts the entire food system
- Corn has pushed animals off the land, then pushed people off the land
- The industrial food chain runs on fossil fuel, not solar energy

### Part 2: Pastoral — Grass
Pollan visits Joel Salatin's Polyface Farm in Virginia's Shenandoah Valley — a "beyond organic" farm that runs on grass and symbiotic relationships between animals. He traces the grass-based food chain from pasture to plate, arguing that grass-fed animals and rotational grazing represent a more sustainable model.

Key findings:
- "Beyond organic" farmers like Salatin reject the industrial organic label
- Grass is the foundation of a sustainable food system — it converts solar energy without fossil fuel
- Polyface Farm uses no chemicals, no confinement, no antibiotics — and is profitable
- The relationship between domesticated animals and humans is mutualism, not exploitation
- Industrial agriculture has created "learned helplessness" in confined animals

### Part 3: Industrial Organic — Big Organic
Pollan examines the organic food movement's journey from grassroots to corporate acquisition. He visits Cascadian Farm (now General Mills) and Earthbound Farm ($350 million organic salad company), showing how "Big Organic" has compromised many of the original principles.

Key findings:
- Organic was co-opted by industrial logic — the USDA's National Organic Program diluted standards
- "Organic" no longer means local, small-scale, or pasture-raised
- Organic produce may travel thousands of miles, undermining the environmental benefit
- Polyphenol-rich organic plants may be healthier than conventionally grown ones
- The tension between Big Organic and Little Organic mirrors the tension in the industrial food chain

### Part 4: The Perfect Meal — Hunter/Gatherer
Pollan hunts wild boar, forages for mushrooms, and gathers sea vegetables to compose a meal entirely from foods he has found or killed himself. This is the meal where the food chain is shortest and most transparent.

Key findings:
- Directly killing and preparing food creates a moral relationship with eating
- The omnivore's dilemma — "what should we eat?" — is answered by nature when you hunt and gather
- Wild foods are more diverse and nutritious than cultivated ones
- Knowing where your food comes from deepens the pleasure of eating

## Key Themes

1. **The Omnivore's Dilemma** — humans evolved to eat everything, which means we must constantly decide what to eat
2. **The Food Chain** — every meal connects you to a chain of ecology, economics, and politics
3. **Corn's Conquest** — a single plant has come to dominate the American food system
4. **Opacity vs Transparency** — the industrial food chain is opaque; the sustainable food chain is transparent
5. **Big vs Small Organic** — the organic movement's compromise with industrial logic
6. **Eating as Ecological Act** — what you eat determines the use you make of the world
7. **The Pleasure of Knowing** — eating with consciousness deepens pleasure
